## B wins

Not on polish — A is the most finished object and C the best engineered — but
on the only question underneath the criteria: B is the one a job seeker at
11pm would love, and the one I was still scrolling after the testing was done.

Three things decided it:

1. **B composed its free sample for the reader; A and C composed theirs for
   the data.** A's signed-out first screen is 40 lines of Databricks; C's free
   portion is the twelve largest companies, and its ask sits 75,000px down
   where almost nobody will meet it (so the measurement the gate exists to
   take will barely collect). B gives 240 roles from 79 companies, newest
   first, every country mixed — a reader anywhere sees a possible life within
   two screens, and the ask arrives exactly where the reading ends, states
   what remains in numbers, and prints its own counter back to the reader
   (`asked 1 · signed in 0 · declined 0`). That last move — the page showing
   you its instrumentation — is the round's single best idea about trust.

2. **B's organizing principle is the reader's question.** "It is late, and
   somebody is hiring" plus bands by *the night we first saw each role* means
   the page answers "what's new for me" first and is different tomorrow — the
   only variant with a reason to come back. A organizes by the volume's
   canonical order, C by company size; both are the data's questions, not the
   reader's.

3. **The metaphor reaches the edges.** The sentence-as-query survives filter
   composition, the empty state ("every word you set is up in the sentence, in
   the lamp colour — put one back and the register comes with it") recovers
   the reader in one click, and the URL is the session, so a find can be sent
   to a friend. A's and C's empty states are a terse line on blank paper, and
   C has no shareable state at all.

B's known frontier — the sentence goes telegraphic in German and Japanese —
is real, and it is the right thing to be wrong about: it fails at the edge of
its best idea, not at its center.

## The best idea in each of the others

**From A — the ask as a suppressible, auditable instrument.** Two parts worth
carrying forward precisely: (1) the ask does not appear when a filtered
register fits — "nine results is an answer, and asking on top of an answer is
a toll"; (2) the bookplate prints every byte the page keeps about the reader,
with a control that burns it. Also portable: the VERIFIED · BOARD READ stamp
that restates its numbers honestly under scope ("185 on the board · 56 in this
scope").

**From C — provenance at the row, and geometry that cannot lie.** C is the
only variant where a role opens *in place* into a full evidence sheet — first
seen, board read, build verdict, funding with "round letter not stated,"
a derivation that names itself, the CIN. And its gauge — depth in roles, exact
because rows are fixed-height — plus the fold that breathes the register
between 371 and 6,423 lines are the only honest answers this round gave to
"make 6,423 feel like abundance, not a wall." Round 2 should steal the
evidence sheet outright.

## What none of them tried

Four gaps visible only with the three side by side. The first is the brief.

1. **No page reads the reader.** All three organize the corpus by the data's
   own structure — company, country, night — and none by the reader's actual
   question: *what could I do?* Nothing anywhere asks (or infers, or lets the
   reader state) what languages they read, whether they need visa sponsorship,
   whether they can relocate — even though the corpus already carries the
   facts to answer (posting language, foreign-hires marks, remote scope), and
   even though the brief's reader in São Paulo or Warsaw lives in a country
   this register doesn't even cover, so *every* role relevant to them is
   remote-or-relocation by definition. B's badges and C's `abroad ✓` token are
   the only gestures. The Japanese reader's dead end is the same gap: type a
   Japanese word, get zero, and no variant explains that the titles are in
   their boards' languages or offers a way through. **Round 2's brief: a
   register that knows who is reading it — a stated (never guessed) reader
   profile of language(s)-read + mobility, a lens that dims to the roles whose
   own facts say "possible for you," and an empty state that bridges the
   language boundary instead of shrugging. Absence stays absence: the lens
   may only use what a posting says.**

2. **Nobody designed the return visit.** The corpus diffs itself nightly and
   B's Tonight band proves the material is there — yet no variant remembers,
   client-side, what the reader saw last time and opens with *their* diff:
   "since you last read: 212 appeared, 179 vanished, 2 in Japan." A page that
   greets a returning reader with what changed for them is the loved-website
   move, and it needs nothing but localStorage.

3. **The reader can keep nothing.** Three read-only registers. No shortlist,
   no saved rows, no "mark this to look at tomorrow" — at 11pm, anxious,
   you *collect*. A client-side shortlist also gives the ask its missing
   honest motive: identity is the one thing that would carry a shortlist
   across devices, so signing in would finally *mean* something the "no"
   button doesn't already give — which is also the only way the gate's
   measurement measures desire for anything real.

4. **All three keyboard cursors are data structures, not attention.** Press
   `j` after scrolling deep and every variant warps back to a cursor that
   never followed the eye. Small, shared, and exactly the kind of tell
   invisible in any one page.

Also noted for the record: A and C both ship the incumbent's nine-select
filter bank essentially verbatim — a shared tell that only B avoided. And the
value proposition of signing in is identically hollow in all three (declining
prints everything, so "yes" buys nothing nameable); B merely words it best.
Gap 3 is the honest repair.

## Round over round

Round 1; nothing to compare. For round 2's baseline: the judge's 70-point bar
now stands at B's 63, and the calibration to beat is not a number — it is that
none of round 1's variants would make a job seeker *tell a friend* about it
unprompted. That, plus the four gaps above, is round 2's brief.
